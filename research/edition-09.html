One less workday. 90 percent never went back.

The Energy Cost

The largest four-day workweek trial to date followed 2,896 employees across 141 organizations in six countries, working one less day at full pay (Fan et al., 2025). Burnout fell. Job satisfaction rose. Mental and physical health improved, and the gains traced to less fatigue, fewer sleep problems, and stronger work ability, not to people squeezing five days of output into four. When the six-month trial ended, 90 percent of the companies kept the shorter week. Here's where AI meets this study. Every hour AI hands back is a fork in the road: refill it with more work, or bank it as recovered life. Most builders refill it without noticing. This research shows what the other choice buys: burnout down, health up, output steady. The trial wasn't about AI, but it answers the question AI keeps raising. You're saving all this time. Spend some of it on being well. If AI runs part of your business, a shorter week isn't a reward for finishing everything. It's the dividend, and you're allowed to collect it.

Twenty years in, AI hasn't dented worker wellbeing.

The Energy Cost

Economists tracked German workers for two decades, from 2000 to 2020, comparing people in AI-exposed jobs with everyone else (Giuntella et al., 2025). The fear this study tested is the one most of us carry quietly: that working alongside automation slowly grinds people down. It didn't show up. AI exposure had no sizeable negative effect on wellbeing or mental health. If anything, health status and health satisfaction improved, largely because the physically punishing parts of jobs shrank. One honest caveat: the data ends in 2020, before generative AI arrived at your desk. Still, it's the longest, cleanest look we have, and it says the tool itself isn't the threat. Your energy isn't being stolen by AI. The real question is who decides where the recovered effort goes. That answer can be you.

AI helps your wellbeing only when it's pointed at the right work.

The Alignment Check

Researchers surveyed 207 companies to test whether adopting AI makes working life better on its own (Valtonen et al., 2025). It doesn't. AI adoption had no direct effect on employee wellbeing. The benefit appeared only indirectly, when AI genuinely optimized tasks and improved safety at work. Adoption is not the intervention. Aim is. This is the same lesson the clinician study taught in Edition 08, now echoed across 207 firms: a tool pointed nowhere in particular does nothing for the human using it. Before you add anything new to your stack, name the task it's relieving you of. If you can't name it, alignment hasn't happened yet, and the wellbeing dividend won't arrive either.
